# Quashing — controlling decisions by issue

Reporter citations are given in the form practitioners use; confirm each on [sci.gov.in](https://www.sci.gov.in/) (search by title; recent judgments carry a neutral citation `YYYY INSC NNN`) before filing. Holdings are one-line summaries, not quotations. Decisions under CrPC s.482 apply to BNSS s.528 — the text of the inherent-powers provision is unchanged.

## The frame

- **State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal**, 1992 Supp (1) SCC 335 — the seven illustrative categories (para 102) in which the FIR or proceeding may be quashed; the power is to be exercised sparingly and in the rarest of rare cases; the court does not embark on an enquiry into the reliability of the allegations.
- **R.P. Kapur v. State of Punjab**, AIR 1960 SC 866 — the earlier formulation: legal bar, allegations not constituting the offence, or no legal evidence at all.
- **State of Karnataka v. L. Muniswamy**, (1977) 2 SCC 699 — inherent power exists to prevent abuse of process and secure the ends of justice; a proceeding that would be an exercise in futility may be quashed.
- **Neeharika Infrastructure Pvt Ltd v. State of Maharashtra**, 2021 INSC 240 — investigation should ordinarily not be thwarted at the FIR stage; "no coercive steps" interim orders are not to be passed routinely and must be reasoned; quashing at the threshold is exceptional; detailed guidelines for High Courts.

## Settlement in non-compoundable offences

- **Gian Singh v. State of Punjab**, (2012) 10 SCC 303 — the High Court may quash non-compoundable proceedings on a settlement where the offence is predominantly private or civil in nature (matrimonial, commercial, family, property); not for heinous offences, offences against society, or where the public interest is involved.
- **Narinder Singh v. State of Punjab**, (2014) 6 SCC 466 — guidelines: the timing of the settlement, the nature of the offence (attempt-to-murder cases need care; settled early with a genuine compromise they may be quashed), the possibility of conviction, and whether continuing would be oppressive.
- **State of Madhya Pradesh v. Laxmi Narayan**, (2019) 5 SCC 688 — restates the limits: offences with a mental-element against society, or by public servants, or heinous offences, are not quashed on compromise; the stage and the conduct of the accused matter.
- **Parbatbhai Aahir v. State of Gujarat**, (2017) 9 SCC 641 — economic offences involving financial and economic well-being of the State, and offences by public servants, stand on a different footing; the court weighs the public interest even where parties have settled.
- **Ramgopal v. State of Madhya Pradesh**, 2021 INSC 571 (verify) — in appropriate private-dispute cases the power may be exercised even after conviction, on a genuine settlement.
- **Shiji v. Radhika**, (2011) 10 SCC 705 — continuing a prosecution after the parties have settled a private dispute may itself be an abuse of process.

## Matrimonial cases and roped-in relatives

- **Preeti Gupta v. State of Jharkhand**, (2010) 7 SCC 667 — caution against omnibus allegations against the husband's relatives in s.498A complaints; vague, general allegations do not sustain a prosecution.
- **Geeta Mehrotra v. State of Uttar Pradesh**, (2012) 10 SCC 741 — relatives named without specific allegations should be released from the proceeding.
- **Kahkashan Kausar v. State of Bihar**, 2022 INSC 165 — general and sweeping allegations against in-laws without specific instances are liable to be quashed.
- **Achin Gupta v. State of Haryana**, 2024 INSC 369 — courts must look at the overall circumstances; a complaint filed as a counterblast after the husband's divorce petition, with vague allegations, warrants quashing; the Court flagged the need to guard against misuse under the BNS as well.
- **Kailashben Mahendrabhai Patel v. State of Maharashtra**, 2024 INSC 737 — omnibus allegations against relatives living separately; quashed.
- **Dara Lakshmi Narayana v. State of Telangana**, 2024 INSC 953 — s.498A cannot be used as a tool for personal vendetta; where the complaint is vague and follows the husband's divorce notice, quashing is warranted; the Court urged caution against implicating family members without specific roles.

## Civil disputes given a criminal colour

- **Indian Oil Corporation v. NEPC India Ltd**, (2006) 6 SCC 736 — a growing tendency to convert purely civil disputes into criminal cases to pressure the opponent; a breach of contract is not cheating unless dishonest intention existed at the inception; but a civil remedy does not by itself bar a criminal prosecution if the ingredients exist.
- **Paramjeet Batra v. State of Uttarakhand**, (2013) 11 SCC 673 — where a dispute is essentially civil and the criminal complaint is a device to pressure, the High Court should quash.
- **Mitesh Kumar J. Sha v. State of Karnataka**, 2021 INSC 648 (verify) — commercial transaction, settlement attempts, and absence of dishonest intention at inception: proceedings quashed.
- **Vesa Holdings Pvt Ltd v. State of Kerala**, (2015) 8 SCC 293 — a mere breach of promise or contract, without more, is not cheating.
- **Naresh Kumar v. State of Karnataka**, 2024 INSC 196 — breach of contract does not become cheating merely because money was not returned; quashed.

## Looking at documents; no mini-trial

- **Rajiv Thapar v. Madan Lal Kapoor**, (2013) 3 SCC 330 — the High Court may consider material of sterling and impeccable quality produced by the accused that is not refuted by the prosecution and which would rule out the allegations; a four-step test.
- **Mohd. Allauddin Khan v. State of Bihar**, (2019) 6 SCC 107 — the High Court cannot weigh evidence or decide disputed questions of fact in a s.482 petition.
- **Mahmood Ali v. State of Uttar Pradesh**, 2023 INSC 684 — where the FIR appears to have been registered with an oblique motive, the court should read between the lines and consider the attendant circumstances.
- **Iqbal v. State of Uttar Pradesh**, 2023 INSC 686 — the court may look at the overall circumstances leading to the FIR, including delay and prior disputes.

## Second FIR, legal bars, sanction

- **T.T. Antony v. State of Kerala**, (2001) 6 SCC 181 — a second FIR on the same cognizable offence arising from the same occurrence is impermissible; the remedy is further investigation under the first.
- **Amitbhai Anilchandra Shah v. CBI**, (2013) 6 SCC 348 — the "same transaction" test for second FIRs.
- **Anil Kumar v. M.K. Aiyappa**, (2013) 10 SCC 705 — a Magistrate cannot order investigation against a public servant under s.156(3) (now s.175) without prior sanction where it is required.
- **Devinder Singh v. State of Punjab**, (2016) 12 SCC 87 — principles on when sanction under s.197 (now s.218) is required: the act must be reasonably connected with official duty.
- **Sarah Mathew v. Institute of Cardio Vascular Diseases**, (2014) 2 SCC 62 — limitation for cognizance (now s.514) runs to the date of filing the complaint, not the date of taking cognizance.

## Summoning orders and complaint cases

- **Pepsi Foods Ltd v. Special Judicial Magistrate**, (1998) 5 SCC 749 — summoning an accused is a serious matter; the order must reflect application of mind; it may be quashed under s.482 or Article 227.
- **Sunil Bharti Mittal v. CBI**, (2015) 4 SCC 609 — a director cannot be summoned merely because of the office held; the complaint must contain specific allegations of the role.
- **Mehmood Ul Rehman v. Khazir Mohammad Tunda**, (2015) 12 SCC 420 — the Magistrate must record satisfaction that a prima facie case exists before issuing process.

## Arrest and investigation hooks

- **Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar**, (2014) 8 SCC 273 — notice under s.41A CrPC (now s.35(3) BNSS) for offences punishable up to seven years; arrest without recorded reasons is impermissible.
- **Lalita Kumari v. Government of Uttar Pradesh**, (2014) 2 SCC 1 — registration of an FIR is mandatory on information disclosing a cognizable offence; preliminary enquiry only in defined categories — relevant when the petitioner argues the FIR was registered without the enquiry the BNSS s.173(3) now permits.
