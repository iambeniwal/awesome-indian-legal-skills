# FIR and police complaints — controlling decisions by issue

Confirm each on [sci.gov.in](https://www.sci.gov.in/) before relying on it. Holdings are one-line summaries, not quotations. Decisions on CrPC ss.154, 155, 156(3), 173 and 200 apply to BNSS ss.173, 174, 175(3), 193 and 223, whose text is materially the same except where noted.

## Registration is mandatory

- **Lalita Kumari v. Government of Uttar Pradesh**, (2014) 2 SCC 1 (Constitution Bench) — registration of an FIR is mandatory when the information discloses a cognizable offence; a preliminary enquiry is permitted only to ascertain whether a cognizable offence is disclosed, in categories such as matrimonial/family disputes, commercial offences, medical negligence, corruption, and cases of abnormal delay; the enquiry should be completed within seven days (the BNSS s.173(3) now provides fourteen days for 3–7-year offences); action against officers who refuse.
- **State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal**, 1992 Supp (1) SCC 335 — the officer in charge must register on information disclosing a cognizable offence; reasonableness or credibility is not a condition precedent.
- **Superintendent of Police, CBI v. Tapan Kumar Singh**, (2003) 6 SCC 175 — the FIR is not an encyclopaedia; it need only disclose information that a cognizable offence has been committed to set the investigation in motion.
- **Ramesh Kumari v. State (NCT of Delhi)**, (2006) 2 SCC 677 — genuineness of the information is for investigation; the officer cannot refuse registration on the ground that it may be false.

## Remedies when the police refuse

- **Sakiri Vasu v. State of Uttar Pradesh**, (2008) 2 SCC 409 — an aggrieved informant should go to the SP under s.154(3) (now s.173(4)) and then to the Magistrate under s.156(3) (now s.175(3)), who has the implied power to direct a proper investigation and to monitor it; writ petitions and s.482 petitions for registration should not be entertained when this remedy exists.
- **Sudhir Bhaskarrao Tambe v. Hemant Yashwant Dhage**, (2016) 6 SCC 277 — reaffirms *Sakiri Vasu*: High Courts should not entertain petitions for FIR registration; the s.156(3) route is the remedy.
- **Priyanka Srivastava v. State of Uttar Pradesh**, (2015) 6 SCC 287 — applications under s.156(3) must be supported by an affidavit so that the applicant is accountable for false averments; the Magistrate must apply judicial mind and may verify; the BNSS s.175(3) now writes the affidavit and the prior s.173(4) step into the statute.
- **Babu Venkatesh v. State of Karnataka**, 2022 INSC 200 — a s.156(3) order passed mechanically without the affidavit and without considering the SP route is unsustainable.
- **Kailash Vijayvargiya v. Rajlakshmi Chaudhuri**, 2023 INSC 489 — the Magistrate must apply mind before directing registration; where a prima facie cognizable offence is disclosed, the direction follows.
- **Om Prakash Ambadkar v. State of Maharashtra**, 2025 INSC 139 (verify) — the Magistrate's discretion under s.156(3) is to be exercised with reasons; the BNSS s.175(3) requires consideration of the police submission.

## Zero FIR and transfer

- **Satvinder Kaur v. State (Govt of NCT of Delhi)**, (1999) 8 SCC 728 — a police station cannot refuse to register on the ground of territorial jurisdiction; it must register and transfer. The BNSS s.173(1) ("irrespective of the area") codifies the zero FIR.
- **Rasiklal Dalpatram Thakkar v. State of Gujarat**, (2010) 1 SCC 1 — the question of territorial jurisdiction is for the investigation and the court, not a reason to refuse registration.

## Closure reports and the informant's rights

- **Bhagwant Singh v. Commissioner of Police**, (1985) 2 SCC 537 — before accepting a closure report the Magistrate must give notice to the informant and hear them; the informant may file a protest petition.
- **Vinubhai Haribhai Malaviya v. State of Gujarat**, (2019) 17 SCC 1 — the Magistrate's power to order further investigation continues till the trial begins.
- **Jakia Nasim Ahesan v. State of Gujarat**, (2011) 12 SCC 302 — the protest petition may be treated as a complaint.

## Complaint cases

- **Hareendran v. Sukumaran**, (2018) 11 SCC 606 — the Magistrate taking cognizance on a complaint must examine the complainant and witnesses; the procedure under ss.200–202 (now ss.223–225) is mandatory.
- **Chandra Deo Singh v. Prokash Chandra Bose**, AIR 1963 SC 1430 — at the pre-process stage the accused has no right to participate (the BNSS proviso to s.223(1) now gives the accused an opportunity of being heard before cognizance — read the current text).

## Arrest, custody and the complainant

- **Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar**, (2014) 8 SCC 273 — arrest is not automatic on registration for offences punishable up to seven years; the complainant should expect a notice procedure (s.35(3) BNSS), not immediate custody.
- **Joginder Kumar v. State of Uttar Pradesh**, (1994) 4 SCC 260 — arrest requires justification beyond the power to arrest.

## Cyber fraud and customer liability

- RBI, *Customer Protection — Limiting Liability of Customers in Unauthorised Electronic Banking Transactions*, circular of 6 July 2017 (DBR.No.Leg.BC.78/09.07.005/2017-18) — zero liability where the fraud is reported within three working days of receiving the communication; limited liability for four to seven days; the bank must credit the amount within ten working days of notification (verify the current master direction).
- The National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal's financial-fraud track (Citizen Financial Cyber Fraud Reporting and Management System) is an administrative mechanism, not a statutory remedy; a police complaint under the BNSS and a bank complaint remain necessary.
